# Skin tone (Monk, Fitzpatrick, individual typology angle)

**Units:** scale category or degrees · **Category:** Demographics

How light or dark a person's skin is, recorded on one of three kinds of scale. Fitzpatrick skin type (I to VI) classifies people by their response to sun exposure, in terms of tanning and burning. The Monk Skin Tone scale is a 10-shade scale of colour swatches that a person or an observer matches to the skin. The individual typology angle (ITA) is a number in degrees calculated from CIELAB lightness (L*) and yellow-blue (b*) readings taken with a colorimeter or spectrophotometer.

The three scales are not interchangeable and sources for different scales are never merged. Fitzpatrick type is defined by sun reaction, yet in studies it is assigned in different ways: by questionnaire, by a dermatologist or reader, or by matching a printed 'perception' of the scale. The Monk scale is a visual match to colour swatches, described in one study as designed to be easy to use for evaluating technology while representing a broad range of skin tones. ITA is an instrument measurement and depends on the device and the body site (sun-exposed sites read lower than sun-protected ones). In one convenience cohort that applied several scales to the same people, categorisation as light, medium or dark was not consistent between scales (evidence.md in the Monk and ITA source folders). This entry holds two Fitzpatrick sources and two documented gaps (Monk, ITA). The NHANES 2003-2004 source is the only probability sample: US adults aged 20 to 59, with Fitzpatrick type assigned from digital photographs by two dermatologist readers who often disagreed, so it is reported separately for each reader, with a third series that averages them by the rule of the published analysis. The Italian source is a near-complete conscription cohort of men aged about 18 in one area in 1990, with phototype I to IV self-reported by questionnaire; types V and VI were not recorded, and 8.9% could not classify themselves. The two Fitzpatrick sources differ in country, age, sex, era and how the type was assigned (readers rating photographs against self-reported sun reaction), and must not be pooled or read as one distribution. The Monk and ITA gaps each record a search on 2026-10-06 that found no population distribution. A fifth source, Heldreth et al. 2024, is a different kind again: self-matching to on-screen swatches in a weighted online survey, reported only as three pooled groups (light 61.8%, medium 23.2%, dark 15.0%). Its groups do not correspond to Fitzpatrick types or Monk points one for one and should not be mapped onto the NHANES reader-assigned shares. A sixth source, He et al. 2014, gives self-reported Fitzpatrick type by self-reported race in a US convenience sample. Set beside the NHANES reader-assigned shares it shows how far the two methods can diverge: among Black respondents 48.4% reported Type VI, while the NHANES readers assigned Type VI to 88.2% and 73.8% of Non-Hispanic Black adults; among White respondents 12.3% reported Type V and 5.5% Type VI, while the readers assigned those types to almost no Non-Hispanic White adults. The samples, years, and race categories differ as well as the method, so this is an illustration that self-reported sun reaction and rated skin colour are different quantities, not a measured difference between them. One study scored the same 4,292 people three ways (O'Hagan et al. 2025, quoted in the Monk gap source's evidence.md): self-reported and dermatologist-rated Fitzpatrick type correlated only moderately (Spearman rho 0.318), and trained annotators' Monk scores tracked the dermatologist's Fitzpatrick rating (rho 0.541) more closely than the person's own (rho 0.299). That is within-person support for treating self-reported and observer-assigned type as different measures. Fitzpatrick type is also sometimes assigned from an instrument: Wilkes et al. 2015 (quoted in the ITA gap source's evidence.md) defined types by melanin-index thresholds, reserving Type VI for readings of 750 or more, after 95.6% of their Black participants described some reaction to the sun. So a stated "Fitzpatrick type" can mean a self-report, an observer's rating, or an instrument threshold, and a source must say which. Sources added later sharpen three points. First, wording: Black respondents asked only about burning and tanning mostly reject the question (59.0% chose "none of the above" in Pichon et al. 2010, a random household sample) or spread across types (48.4% Type VI in He et al. 2014), while a version whose options name a skin colour puts 71.1% at Type VI (Sommers et al. 2019). Second, rater: in the SCIN data the same contributors were typed by themselves and by a dermatologist, and the two agree for 31.1%; two pools of annotators giving Monk scores to the same photographs agree for 40.1%. Third, instrument readings overlap heavily between self-reported types: mean inner-arm L* falls from 68.0 at Type I to 42.8 at Type VI in Sommers et al., with SDs of 8.5 to 10.1 at Types III to V. The Monk and ITA rows are still gaps for any defined population; the SCIN Monk shares and the digitised ITA categories from Wilkes et al. 2015 are volunteer or workplace groups.

## Self-reported Fitzpatrick phototype I to IV, men aged about 18 at the military medical visit, Abruzzo and Ascoli Piceno (Italy), 1990

*Guidance: draft, not yet expert-reviewed*

**Origin:** transcribed from a publication · **Data:** [data.csv](../metrics/skin_tone/sources/fitzpatrick_central_italy_men_18_19_1999/data.csv)

### Read this before using the numbers

- **Phototypes V and VI were not recorded.** The paper says "Since all subjects were Caucasians, phototype V and VI were not recorded." The four shares sum to 100% of a scale cut off at IV.
- **The abstract's percentages use a different denominator.** The abstract's "44.5%" for phototype IV and "10.3%" for I or II are shares of all 5,837 men, including 519 with no usable answer. Among the 5,318 who answered, the shares are 48.9% and 11.3% (2.7% plus 8.6%). This entry uses the valid-answer shares.
- **8.9% could not be classified.** 519 men did not know their sensitivity to the sun or gave more than one answer.
- **Men only, one birth cohort, one area.** The paper says the group "was not intended to represent all of Italy but only the regions indicated."
- **Not a designed probability sample, but close to a census.** The men called were about 88% of 18-year-old residents, and those examined were the ones scheduled for early-morning visits, which the paper describes as random. Sampling is recorded as 'other'.
- **Self-report has a subjective component.** The authors write that "the reliability is always questionable due to an inherent margin of error."

### Population and method

- Men born in 1972 (a small percentage in 1971) called for the preliminary military medical visit at Chieti during 1990. The 11,862 men called are described as approximately 88% of the 18-year-old residents of the region of Abruzzo and the province of Ascoli Piceno (Marche). The 5,837 scheduled for an early-morning visit had the dermatological examination; 5,318 of them gave a usable phototype answer.
- Sample: 5,318 of 5,837 eligible. Sampling: other. Exclusions: 519 of the 5,837 (8.9%) were excluded from the phototype analysis because they did not know their sensitivity to the sun or gave more than one answer. No one declined to participate. Participants are described as healthy.
- **Method:** Self-report. Each man completed a questionnaire before the physical examination, reporting his skin's response to an initial 45 to 60 minutes of midday summer sun: burning after 24 hours and tanning after 7 days. Answers were classified by Fitzpatrick's sun-reactive criteria. Phototypes V and VI were not recorded, the paper says, "Since all subjects were Caucasians".
- **Device:** None. Self-completed questionnaire.
- **Protocol:** At least one examiner was present to explain the questionnaire, only when asked, while it was being answered.
- The four shares are the paper's "Valid %" column in Table 1: the share of the 5,318 men with a usable answer, converted from percent to a fraction (48.9% becomes 0.489). Nothing else was computed.
- The same table also gives each type as a share of all 5,837 men, including the 519 without a usable answer: 2.5%, 7.8%, 36.3% and 44.5%. Those are the figures quoted in the paper's abstract.
- The paper reports no weights and no confidence intervals.

## Self-reported Fitzpatrick phototype I to VI by self-reported race, US adults in a convenience survey enriched for minority groups (He et al. 2014)

*Guidance: draft, not yet expert-reviewed*

**Origin:** transcribed from a publication · **Data:** [data.csv](../metrics/skin_tone/sources/he_2014_us_self_reported_fitzpatrick_by_race/data.csv)

### Read this before using the numbers

- **The overall distribution reflects who was recruited.** The authors write: "Our study enriched for responses from ethnic minorities and does not fully represent the demographics of the US population." The sample is 30.2% White, 17.0% Asian, and 16.4% Black as printed in Table II.
- **Convenience sample within each group too.** Social media, chain referral, and community events around one medical centre; 69% women.
- **This is sun reaction, not skin colour.** The paper reports that "the entire range of FSPT (I-VI) was observed in each racial group", that "61 blacks (11%) also reported burning moderately or very easily", and that "401 whites (39%) reported tanning moderately or profusely".
- **One question, not validated.** Phototype comes from a single self-report item, and the authors state the survey was not validated for reliability.
- **Race categories are the survey's own**, with Latino offered as a race option and a separate multiracial group.
- **Small inconsistencies in the printed table.** The Latino row's counts sum to 340 against a printed N of 342, and the race Ns sum to 3,379 of 3,386.

### Population and method

- 3,386 adults who answered an internet or paper survey over six months. The link was spread through social media, including pages for minority groups, and by participants among their acquaintances; paper versions in English and Spanish were handed out at community events and at seminars at the University of California, San Francisco. 69% were women and the mean age was 37.0 years.
- Sample: 3,386. Sampling: convenience sample. Exclusions: Individuals younger than 18 years. No other exclusion is stated.
- **Method:** Self-report. Phototype was taken from the participant's answer to "How does your skin react if you go outside without sunscreen for half an hour?", with six options from "Always burns easily" and "Never tan" (I) to "Never burns" and "Tans profusely" (VI). The paper states "There was no physician assessment of skin phototype for study participants."
- **Device:** None. Internet or paper questionnaire.
- **Protocol:** Not stated beyond the questionnaire item. The authors note the survey "was not validated for reliability".
- Race-group shares are the row percentages printed in the paper's Table II, converted to fractions (48.4% becomes 0.484). The overall shares are from the Results text. Nothing else was computed.
- The paper reports no weights and no confidence intervals for these shares.
- Table II also breaks phototype down by sex, age, and self-reported skin, hair, and eye colour. Those rows are not entered here.

## Self-reported light, medium, or dark skin tone, US adults in a weighted online survey, 2021 (Heldreth et al. 2024)

*Guidance: draft, not yet expert-reviewed*

**Origin:** transcribed from a publication · **Data:** [data.csv](../metrics/skin_tone/sources/heldreth_2024_us_online_survey_light_medium_dark/data.csv)

### Read this before using the numbers

- **Three different scales are pooled.** A respondent counted as "medium" chose Fitzpatrick III or IV, or Monk 4 to 6, or Fenty 200 to 390, depending on which scale they were shown. The paper does not show that these cut-points are equivalent.
- **The grouping is the authors' own.** The paper does not test other cut-points on any of the three scales.
- **Online panel, not a probability sample.** The paper says "The random sample was not nationally representative on entry, but data was weighted to be nationally representative on ethnoracial group and gender."
- **The weighted sample is young.** 30.9% of the weighted sample is aged 18 to 29; age was not used for weighting.
- **Self-matched on an uncalibrated screen.** The authors report that desktop versus mobile made no significant difference to their main outcome, which is not the same as showing the colour match is accurate.
- **Two printed percentages do not match their counts.** In Table 1 the unweighted shares for medium and dark are printed as 27.1% and 28.0%, but 582 and 638 of 2,214 are 26.3% and 28.8%. The weighted shares used here cannot be checked from the paper.
- **The paper's own table marks national data on skin tone as "no data available".**

### Population and method

- 2,214 adults in the United States who completed an online survey through the Qualtrics platform in April to May 2021. Respondents were screened to quotas based on Census population proportions, with respondents reporting medium and dark skin tones oversampled, then weighted by raking to the 2019 American Community Survey on race and gender.
- Sample: 2,214. Sampling: convenience sample. Exclusions: Under 18; not agreeing to answer questions collecting potentially sensitive information. No other exclusion is stated.
- **Method:** Self-report. Each respondent was randomly assigned one of three on-screen scales (a 6-swatch Fitzpatrick graphic, the 10-point Monk scale, or a 40-shade Fenty Beauty palette) and asked "Which of the following is closest to your skin tone?" The authors then grouped answers into light (Fitzpatrick I and II, Monk 1 to 3, Fenty 100 to 190), medium (Fitzpatrick III and IV, Monk 4 to 6, Fenty 200 to 390), and dark (Fitzpatrick V and VI, Monk 7 to 10, Fenty 400 to 490).
- **Device:** The respondent's own desktop or mobile screen; no colour calibration.
- **Protocol:** Respondents were asked to hold the back of their hand up to the screen until they found the colour that most closely matched.
- The three shares are the paper's "Weighted % of Sample" from Table 1, converted from percent to a fraction (61.8% becomes 0.618). Nothing else was computed.
- The weights adjust for race and gender only. Skin tone, age, and education are listed in the table under "Not used for weighting".
- The paper gives no confidence interval for these shares.
- The unweighted counts are 994 light, 582 medium, and 638 dark.

## NHANES 2003-2004, Fitzpatrick skin type assigned by two dermatologist image readers, adults aged 20-59

*Guidance: draft, not yet expert-reviewed*

**Origin:** recomputed by this project from public data · **Data:** [data.csv](../metrics/skin_tone/sources/nhanes_2003_2004_fitzpatrick_image_readers/data.csv)

### Read this before using the numbers

- **The two readers often disagree.** Among the 2,691 people given a type from 1 to 6 by both readers, they gave the same type for 1,657 (61.6%) and a type within one step for 2,551 (94.8%). Their distributions differ: for all adults, Type III is 71.4% by Reader 1 and 46.1% by Reader 2. Neither reader is known to be right.
- **Type was assigned from photographs by readers, not from a person's reported reaction to sun and not by instrument.** The documentation defines the Fitzpatrick type by response to sun exposure, but the readers saw digital images. The documentation does not say what the readers were told to judge from, who they were, or which images they used for type (Not stated).
- **The consensus value is not used.** An image went to a third consensus reading only if the pair of types was a "reject" in the documentation's matrix (one reader in I to III, the other in IV to VI), a critical value was missing, or a condition needed follow-up; the codebook counts 260 consensus readings. Most disagreements between the two readers (for example Type II against Type III) were accepted as they stood and have no consensus value. This source does not report the consensus value.
- **Ages 20 to 59 only.** The skin photograph exam was for adults in that age range.
- **The data are from 2003-2004.** Population composition and sun behaviour may have changed since.
- **People examined but not read are left out, with no adjustment.** Of 2,992 examined adults aged 20 to 59, 296 have no type from either reader, and 3 (Reader 1) and 5 (Reader 2) were coded 9, "Not determined, picture missing". The weights were not adjusted for them.
- **The survey's own documentation says the US distribution was unknown.** It states: "Currently, the distribution of skin type across the U.S. population is unknown." These figures are an analysis of that survey by this project; no published distribution was available to check against.
- **Race and ethnicity groups are NHANES's own 2003-2004 categories** (RIDRETH1), self-reported. The fifth group is "Other Race - Including Multi-Racial"; Asian people are not a separate group. Some groups are small (Other Hispanic: 97 people for each reader).
- **The published single-score distribution depends on an unstated rounding rule.** Keiser et al. 2012 averaged the two readers and report 47.8% at score 3 and 33.1% at score 2. That matches the raw data only if half-point averages are rounded down, which moves people toward lighter types. This entry reports each reader separately and, as a third series, the averaged score with that rounding rule.
- **The published analysis calls this a "digital image skin type" score, lightest to darkest**, not a Fitzpatrick sun-reactivity type, although the NHANES variable is labelled Fitzpatrick skin type. Treat it as a reader's rating of skin colour from photographs of the trunk and limbs.

### Population and method

- US adults aged 20 to 59 examined in the NHANES 2003-2004 mobile examination centre, which took digital skin photographs of a full sample of adults in that age range. Each reader's type is reported separately, so the people counted differ slightly by reader.
- Sample: 2,693 of 2,992 eligible. Sampling: probability sample. Exclusions: The documentation says: "Sample persons aged 20-59 years who do not meet any of the exclusion criteria." and "A person using a wheelchair did not receive the photos of the back or back of the legs. He/she would be eligible for the other photos. There are no safety exclusions for this component." Here, examined adults with no type from a reader (code 9, or no entry) are left out of that reader's table; n is the number of people with a type from at least one reader.
- **Method:** Fitzpatrick skin type I to VI assigned by dermatologist readers from digital photographs (NHANES variables DEX1FITZ for Reader 1 and DEX2FITZ for Reader 2). The documentation says: "The dermatology digital images were read by two separate dermatologists." Who the two readers were, how they were trained, whether the same two read every image, and what they were told to judge the type from: Not stated. It defines the type as "a method of classifying individuals based on their response to sun exposure in terms of tanning and burning (photosensitivity)" and lists "I - Always burns, never tans (pale white skin)" through "VI - Never burns, (deeply pigmented dark brown to black skin)". The page does not say how a reaction to sun was inferred from a photograph. Code 9 means, in the codebook's words, "Not determined, picture missing" (3 people for Reader 1, 5 for Reader 2). Consensus rule, quoted: "When the finding of the two readers disagreed, the image reading software program required a third consensus reading." and "Any outcome that resulted in "reject" went to consensus." In the documentation's matrix for Fitzpatrick type, a pair of types both in I to III, or both in IV to VI, is "Accept"; any pair with one reader in I to III and the other in IV to VI is "Reject". Missing critical values and any condition requiring follow-up also sent an image to consensus. The consensus value (DEX6FITZ, "Reader 6") exists for 260 people (codebook count) and is not used in this source. Keiser et al. 2012 (see verification) add detail the NHANES page does not give: "Two board-certified dermatologists independently reviewed each digital image to assign a skin type (DIST) score on a scale of 1 to 6 (1 = lightest and 6 = darkest)", from images "of their back, arms, hands, and lower extremities" with "no images ... taken of the participant's face, eyes, or hair", and "Dermatologists received validation training on skin type scoring."
- **Device:** Photographs taken by a health technician using "a Kodak DCS760 digital camera with a 50mm lens". The skin type itself was assigned by the readers, not by an instrument.
- **Protocol:** "The exam consists of four digital images": "BACK with ELBOWS", "INNER ARM" (left arm extended, palm up), "FRONT OF LEGS with HANDS" and "BACK OF LEGS with PALMS". The readers also assessed image quality. Which of the four images, and so which body sites, each reader used to assign the Fitzpatrick type: Not stated.
- Reader 1 and Reader 2 are reported separately. No single combined or consensus type is made, because the two readers disagree often (see the reader agreement below).
- Proportions are weighted with the examination weight (WTMEC2YR); this is one 2-year cycle, so the weight is not halved. Each reader's shares are the weighted number of people given that type divided by the weighted number of people given any type (1 to 6) by that reader.
- 95% intervals are the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the share across 500 bootstrap resamples of primary sampling units within strata (Rao-Wu), built once on everyone examined in the 2003-2004 file (9,643 people of all ages) and reused for each subgroup, so they reflect the survey's clustered design.
- n is the number of people in the subgroup with a type from that reader; it is the same on all six rows of a subgroup. Subgroups with fewer than 50 people are not reported. Race and ethnicity is NHANES variable RIDRETH1; there is no age split.
- Code 9 ("Not determined, picture missing") and a blank entry are both treated as no type from that reader. Of the 2,992 examined adults aged 20 to 59, Reader 1 gave a type to 2,693 and Reader 2 to 2,691; 296 have a blank entry for both readers.
- Reader agreement, unweighted, among the 2,691 people given a type from 1 to 6 by both readers: the two readers gave the same type to 1,657 (61.6%) and a type within one step of each other for 2,551 (94.8%). Of those 2,691 people, 212 had one reader in types I to III and the other in IV to VI, the combinations the documentation's matrix sends to consensus. Counting all 2,696 people with a value (1 to 6 or 9) from both readers, 1,660 had the same value, including 3 people given code 9 by both.
- No adjustment was made for examined adults who were not read, and no reweighting for the difference between the two readers.
- A third series, "Both readers averaged, half-points rounded down", gives one score per person for the 2,691 adults with a type from both readers. It follows Keiser et al. 2012, whose published counts it reproduces exactly. Rounding a half-point down moves people toward lighter types; rounding up would put 68.4% of all adults at Type III instead of 47.8%.

## Self-reported Fitzpatrick type I to IV with a "none of the above" option, random household sample of Black adults, California (Pichon et al. 2010)

*Guidance: draft, not yet expert-reviewed*

**Origin:** transcribed from a publication · **Data:** [data.csv](../metrics/skin_tone/sources/pichon_2010_california_black_adults_fitzpatrick_with_none/data.csv)

### Read this before using the numbers

- **59% said none of the options described them.** Under the usual scoring they would be dropped as non-responders, which raises the apparent share of Type IV from 26.8% to 65.5%.
- **Only four types were offered.** The questionnaire used the original four sun-reactive types.
- **The non-responders differ.** The paper reports that those who chose "none" had lower incomes than types III and IV, included more men, and were least likely to use sunscreen.
- **Unweighted.** Counties were sampled in proportion to their share of the state's Black population, but no weights are reported.
- **A small inconsistency in the table.** The four type counts sum to 854; the table heads that column N=853.

### Population and method

- 2,085 Black adult residents of California, aged 18 to 95 (mean 43.8), 57.9% women, reached by a three-stage random household sample: the seven counties where most California Black residents live, then randomly selected high- and low-segregation census tracts, then all households in randomly selected block groups. One self-identified Black adult per household.
- Sample: 2,085. Sampling: probability sample. Exclusions: None stated. The paper reports that 99% of those who answered the door completed the survey; it does not report how many households did not answer.
- **Method:** Self-report on a written survey. The question was "Which of the following best describes your skin's usual reaction to your first exposure to summer sun, without sunscreen, for one-half hour at midday?" with four Fitzpatrick options (always burn, unable to tan; usually burn, then can tan if I work at it; sometimes mild burn, then tan easily; rarely burn, tan easily) and a fifth added for this study: "none of the above describes me".
- **Device:** None. Written questionnaire.
- **Protocol:** Surveys were handed out door to door on weekends by Black surveyors from a community organisation; participants received $10.
- The five shares are the paper's "% of sample" with the "none apply" option, from Table 1, converted to fractions (59.0% becomes 0.590). Nothing else was computed.
- The same table gives the shares among only the 41% who chose a type, the way the scale is normally scored: 6.0%, 6.4%, 22.1%, and 65.5% for types I to IV. Those are not the entered values.
- The paper reports no sampling weights and no confidence intervals for these shares.

## Fitzpatrick type, self-reported and dermatologist-estimated for the same contributors, SCIN dermatology image dataset, United States, 2023

*Guidance: draft, not yet expert-reviewed*

**Origin:** recomputed by this project from public data · **Data:** [data.csv](../metrics/skin_tone/sources/scin_2023_fitzpatrick_self_and_dermatologist/data.csv)

### Read this before using the numbers

- **People with a skin concern who answered an advertisement.** Not a sample of any population. Among those who gave demographics the dataset authors report more women and more people under 40 than the US population, and race or ethnicity is missing for nearly half of contributions.
- **Shares are unweighted and have no intervals.** An interval would describe only the size of this volunteer group, not how well it represents anyone else.
- **Rated from contributors' own phone photographs of a skin condition**, taken in uncontrolled light and often of affected skin.
- **The two ratings disagree for most people.** They match for 31% of contributors rated both ways. Among Black or African American contributors, 34.8% report Type VI themselves while dermatologists assign Type VI to 10.3%; among White contributors, 9.7% report Type V or VI and dermatologists assign those types to 1.3%.

- **The two ratings cover different subsets.** Fewer than half of contributions have a self-report; most have a dermatologist estimate.

### Population and method

- Volunteer contributions to the SCIN dermatology image dataset: internet users in the United States who were shown a search advertisement against a dermatology-related query on a mobile device and chose to submit photographs of a skin concern. 5,033 contributions.
- Sample: 4,302 of 5,033 eligible. Sampling: convenience sample. Exclusions: Self-reported: 2,530 contributions left the question blank and 328 answered that none of the options fit, leaving 2,175. Dermatologist: 731 contributions have no label, leaving 4,302.
- **Method:** Two ratings, reported separately. Self-reported: the dataset documentation says "users were asked questions about how their skin responds to sun exposure and those responses were mapped to these FST values". Dermatologist: "Label from a dermatologist estimating the Fitzpatrick skin type" from the contribution's images; where a contribution has more than one such label, the first is used.
- **Device:** None. Questionnaire for the self-report; visual rating of photographs for the dermatologist estimate.
- **Protocol:** Not stated in the dataset documentation beyond the descriptions quoted under method.
- Shares are the unweighted fraction at each type among contributions with that rating, overall and for self-reported race and ethnicity groups with at least 50 such contributions.
- 328 of the 2,503 contributors who answered the self-report question (13.1%) said none of the options fit. They are not in the shares.
- Agreement, among 1,878 contributions with both ratings: the same type for 31.1%, and within one type for 73.1%.

## Skin lightness (CIELAB L*) on the inner upper arm, by self-reported Fitzpatrick type, women in Philadelphia and San Juan (Sommers et al. 2019)

*Guidance: draft, not yet expert-reviewed*

**Origin:** transcribed from a publication · **Data:** [data.csv](../metrics/skin_tone/sources/sommers_2019_lstar_by_self_reported_fitzpatrick/data.csv)

### Read this before using the numbers

- **Women only, recruited by flyer, in two cities.** Enrolment was matched to the age and race mix of a sexual assault registry, not to any population.
- **The paper gives two sample sizes.** The abstract says N=466; the Methods, Results, and tables say 446. 466 is also given as the number enrolled.
- **Conditional on self-report.** Each row describes the women who chose that type, and the type descriptions named a skin colour, which ties the answer to lightness more than a sun-reaction question would.
- **Self-report tracks lightness across the whole sample but not within groups.** The paper reports a correlation of -0.77 between type and L* overall, but -0.23 among Black or Black Hispanic women.
- **L* values in the text are on a different scale.** The Results quote L* means such as 6361.9, a hundred times the table's scale; the table values are used.

### Population and method

- 446 English- or Spanish-speaking women aged 21 and over in Philadelphia and San Juan, Puerto Rico, recruited by flyers at health sciences centres for a study of skin injury, and enrolled to match the age and race and ethnicity mix of an existing sexual assault registry. Mean age 32.8.
- Sample: 446. Sampling: convenience sample. Exclusions: Women with injury to the genitalia. Of 575 screened, 109 declined.
- **Method:** CIELAB L* (lightness; the paper gives a general skin range of about 20, dark, to 85, light) measured with a reflectance spectrophotometer, reported as mean and SD within each self-reported Fitzpatrick type. Self-reported type, chosen in an interview from six descriptions that name a skin colour as well as a sun reaction: "Type I, very fair, always burns, never tans; Type II, fair, burns easily; Type III, fair, sometimes burns; Type IV, beige-olive, burns minimally; Type V, moderate brown, rarely burns; Type VI, dark brown or black, never burns".
- **Device:** ColorTec PSM reflectance spectrophotometer.
- **Protocol:** Inner upper arm, two inches distal from the axilla, chosen as a sun-protected site. The paper states that quality control kept L* error within 5%.
- Means and SDs are transcribed from the L* row of the paper's Table 1; n for each type is from the table's whole-sample row. Units are L* units, not degrees of ITA.
- These are distributions of an instrument reading conditional on the person's own type choice. The paper does not give the L* distribution of the whole sample or of each race group.

Measured from Figure, panel B (ITA series, the darker bars). Precision: about one participant per bar (0.2 percentage points). Method: Page 902 of the article PDF rendered at 600 dpi; bar tops, including their outlines, located in pixels and scaled against the gridlines, which are 25 participants apart. ### Read this before using the numbers

- **Measured from a chart, not read from a table.** Each count is good to about one participant. Two bars measured almost exactly half-way between whole numbers (45.6 and 52.6), so those two counts could each be one lower.
- **One workplace, two weeks.** The paper does not say how participants were recruited on the campus.
- **Category cut-points are not stated in this paper.** It refers to an earlier publication for them.
- **The figure was corrected after publication.** The article notes a correction "to fix statistics in the Figure"; the corrected version is the one measured.

### Population and method

- 556 people "drawn from the Council for Scientific and Industrial Research campus in Pretoria, South Africa". They self-identified as black (390), Indian/Asian (51), white (99), or colored (16).
- Sample: 556. Sampling: convenience sample. Exclusions: Not stated. Participants gave written consent and spoke English.
- **Method:** Individual typology angle (ITA) measured with a colorimeter and grouped into six categories (very light, light, intermediate, tanned, brown, dark) following Del Bino and Bernerd 2013, which the paper cites for the cut-points without restating them.
- **Device:** Skin Colorimeter CL 400 (Courage+Khazaka Electronics).
- **Protocol:** Device held against the inner part of the upper non-dominant arm, after the participant cleaned the arm with a sanitary wipe.
- The paper gives the number of participants in each ITA category only as a bar chart. The six bar heights were measured in pixels against the chart's gridlines and rounded to whole participants: 58, 46, 13, 53, 348, 38.
- Shares are those counts divided by their total. The paper reports no ITA mean, SD, or percentile.
