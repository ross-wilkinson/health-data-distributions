| 1 | [Resting heart rate and pulse](entries/resting-heart-rate.md) | Cardiovascular | available | NHANES 2015-2018, clinic pulse; Quer et al. 2020, Fitbit wearable cohort (92,457 US adults), daily resting heart rate; Fitbit daily resting heart rate by sex, 92,457 US adults, 2016-2018 (Quer et al. 2020, distribution digitised from Fig 2) | The two sources measure different quantities in different people and must not be merged. NHANES is a pulse counted for 30 seconds by an examiner after 5 minutes of seated rest in a clinic, in a probability sample of US adults aged 18 and over (2015-2018, weighted, no exclusions for medication or conditions). The Fitbit cohort (Quer et al. 2020) is a daily value from a proprietary algorithm that is meant to represent a supine rate on waking, taken in normal living conditions and averaged over many days per person, in a convenience sample of device owners (2016-2018, about 63% women, average age 45.8, unweighted). In the two data files the NHANES mean is 72.41 beats/min (median 72.0) and the Fitbit cohort mean is 65.5 beats/min, so the wearable mean is the lower of the two. Method, timing and population all differ, and the two files do not separate their effects, so the gap cannot be attributed to the device or the method alone. The Fitbit source records only a mean, so no other central or spread values can be compared. A third source gives the Fitbit cohort's distribution by sex, measured from the paper's Fig 2 and labelled as digitised: medians of 62.9 beats/min for men and 66.4 for women, against NHANES clinic-pulse medians of 70 and 72. The same cautions apply, and the digitised percentiles describe differences between people's long-run averages, not day-to-day values. |

| 6 | [Skin tone (Monk, Fitzpatrick, individual typology angle)](entries/skin-tone.md) | Demographics | available | Self-reported Fitzpatrick phototype I to IV, men aged about 18 at the military medical visit, Abruzzo and Ascoli Piceno (Italy), 1990; Self-reported Fitzpatrick phototype I to VI by self-reported race, US adults in a convenience survey enriched for minority groups (He et al. 2014); Self-reported light, medium, or dark skin tone, US adults in a weighted online survey, 2021 (Heldreth et al. 2024); Individual typology angle and other colorimeter measures: no population distribution found in this search; Monk Skin Tone scale: no population distribution found in this search; NHANES 2003-2004, Fitzpatrick skin type assigned by two dermatologist image readers, adults aged 20-59; Self-reported Fitzpatrick type I to IV with a "none of the above" option, random household sample of Black adults, California (Pichon et al. 2010); Fitzpatrick type, self-reported and dermatologist-estimated for the same contributors, SCIN dermatology image dataset, United States, 2023; Monk Skin Tone assigned from photographs by trained lay annotators, SCIN dermatology image dataset, United States, 2023; Skin lightness (CIELAB L*) on the inner upper arm, by self-reported Fitzpatrick type, women in Philadelphia and San Juan (Sommers et al. 2019); Self-reported Fitzpatrick type I to VI by race group, using descriptions that name skin colour, women in Philadelphia and San Juan (Sommers et al. 2019); ITA skin colour category measured by colorimeter, staff of one research campus, Pretoria, South Africa, 2014 (Wilkes et al. 2015, digitised) | The three scales are not interchangeable and sources for different scales are never merged. Fitzpatrick type is defined by sun reaction, yet in studies it is assigned in different ways: by questionnaire, by a dermatologist or reader, or by matching a printed 'perception' of the scale. The Monk scale is a visual match to colour swatches, described in one study as designed to be easy to use for evaluating technology while representing a broad range of skin tones. ITA is an instrument measurement and depends on the device and the body site (sun-exposed sites read lower than sun-protected ones). In one convenience cohort that applied several scales to the same people, categorisation as light, medium or dark was not consistent between scales (evidence.md in the Monk and ITA source folders). This entry holds two Fitzpatrick sources and two documented gaps (Monk, ITA). The NHANES 2003-2004 source is the only probability sample: US adults aged 20 to 59, with Fitzpatrick type assigned from digital photographs by two dermatologist readers who often disagreed, so it is reported separately for each reader, with a third series that averages them by the rule of the published analysis. The Italian source is a near-complete conscription cohort of men aged about 18 in one area in 1990, with phototype I to IV self-reported by questionnaire; types V and VI were not recorded, and 8.9% could not classify themselves. The two Fitzpatrick sources differ in country, age, sex, era and how the type was assigned (readers rating photographs against self-reported sun reaction), and must not be pooled or read as one distribution. The Monk and ITA gaps each record a search on 2026-10-06 that found no population distribution. A fifth source, Heldreth et al. 2024, is a different kind again: self-matching to on-screen swatches in a weighted online survey, reported only as three pooled groups (light 61.8%, medium 23.2%, dark 15.0%). Its groups do not correspond to Fitzpatrick types or Monk points one for one and should not be mapped onto the NHANES reader-assigned shares. A sixth source, He et al. 2014, gives self-reported Fitzpatrick type by self-reported race in a US convenience sample. Set beside the NHANES reader-assigned shares it shows how far the two methods can diverge: among Black respondents 48.4% reported Type VI, while the NHANES readers assigned Type VI to 88.2% and 73.8% of Non-Hispanic Black adults; among White respondents 12.3% reported Type V and 5.5% Type VI, while the readers assigned those types to almost no Non-Hispanic White adults. The samples, years, and race categories differ as well as the method, so this is an illustration that self-reported sun reaction and rated skin colour are different quantities, not a measured difference between them. One study scored the same 4,292 people three ways (O'Hagan et al. 2025, quoted in the Monk gap source's evidence.md): self-reported and dermatologist-rated Fitzpatrick type correlated only moderately (Spearman rho 0.318), and trained annotators' Monk scores tracked the dermatologist's Fitzpatrick rating (rho 0.541) more closely than the person's own (rho 0.299). That is within-person support for treating self-reported and observer-assigned type as different measures. Fitzpatrick type is also sometimes assigned from an instrument: Wilkes et al. 2015 (quoted in the ITA gap source's evidence.md) defined types by melanin-index thresholds, reserving Type VI for readings of 750 or more, after 95.6% of their Black participants described some reaction to the sun. So a stated "Fitzpatrick type" can mean a self-report, an observer's rating, or an instrument threshold, and a source must say which. Sources added later sharpen three points. First, wording: Black respondents asked only about burning and tanning mostly reject the question (59.0% chose "none of the above" in Pichon et al. 2010, a random household sample) or spread across types (48.4% Type VI in He et al. 2014), while a version whose options name a skin colour puts 71.1% at Type VI (Sommers et al. 2019). Second, rater: in the SCIN data the same contributors were typed by themselves and by a dermatologist, and the two agree for 31.1%; two pools of annotators giving Monk scores to the same photographs agree for 40.1%. Third, instrument readings overlap heavily between self-reported types: mean inner-arm L* falls from 68.0 at Type I to 42.8 at Type VI in Sommers et al., with SDs of 8.5 to 10.1 at Types III to V. The Monk and ITA rows are still gaps for any defined population; the SCIN Monk shares and the digitised ITA categories from Wilkes et al. 2015 are volunteer or workplace groups. |
